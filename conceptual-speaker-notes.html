Collective agency — speaker notes

15 minutes presenting, 5 minutes discussing. Two optional backups.

01 · When is a collective an agent?

45 seconds. The question is when we gain explanatory power by treating a collection as an agent. There are three connected issues: agency in general, collective agency, and human agency within a collective. This talk focuses on the second. The companion talk asks about human control. We now have a stronger starting point than a chain of related ideas: Hammond and coauthors already formalize collective agency using causal abstraction. I want to ask what predictive organization adds to that framework, and what comparison could show that it adds nothing. This is a research proposal with a checked illustration, not a completed empirical result. The cover is a conceptual illustration, not data.

02 · The question to investigate

75 seconds. Consider a team whose routine decisions are easy to forecast. That forecast alone does not tell us how the team will react to a new incentive, a changed communication rule, or a member challenging the objective. A collective-agent model should explain some of these responses through a relatively economical account of goals and adaptation. The proposed comparison asks whether a macrodescription selected for predictive compression also works for these interventions. We should restrict the goals and intervention maps before evaluating outcomes; otherwise we can fit a story to almost anything. The point is to compare explanations on the same systems, with held-out changes.

03 · Different claims need different evidence

90 seconds. These are not rungs of one agreed definition. Computational mechanics studies predictive organization. Kenton and colleagues focus on policies adapting when the consequences of actions change. Barandiaran, Di Paolo, and Rohde ask for autonomous organization and norms grounded in it. List and Pettit emphasize robust rational representational and goal states in group agents. A reward-maximizing controller can fit a behavioral account without demonstrating endogenous norms. A group can satisfy a rationality model while members have little influence. Our main operational comparison uses behavioral agency and explicitly leaves the stronger autonomy and institutional questions open. Ask the group which account they want the model to explain.

04 · What closure and blankets give us

75 seconds. Predictive causal states group histories by their future distributions. Informational closure asks whether knowing the micro past adds anything to predicting the macro future once the macro past is known. That can make a macrodescription scientifically useful. It does not establish an unrestricted intervention result, and a constant macrovariable passes trivially. Causal blankets identify minimal directional statistics for a chosen system–environment split. They are available for each bipartition, so existence alone does not identify the right agent. Bruineberg and colleagues give a related conceptual caution about treating statistical blankets as worldly boundaries. This is a caution about interpretation, not a refutation of the causal-blanket construction.

05 · From a stream to a world model

75 seconds. AI in a vat gives us the action-conditioned bridge. Instead of only predicting a stream, a world model represents observations under possible action sequences. This is already established work and should not be presented as our innovation. It still fixes a controlled generator. In an organization, a rule change can cause participants to change the policies they use. Hammond and colleagues distinguish interventions with policies held fixed from interventions known when policies are selected. The distinction is about the information available to policy selection, not simply whether an event happens early or late. We need to include that mechanism response in what the proposed macro model predicts.

06 · Collective agency already has a formal model

90 seconds. Causal Foundations of Collective Agency is the closest prior work. It formalizes mechanized abstraction: map micro variables to macro variables, map interventions, and require agreement of the induced outcome distributions. The framework can have multiple equilibria, hence sets of distributions rather than one prediction. It then asks whether the macro mechanism behaves rationally relative to a utility and rationality relation. The paper proves an actor–critic example and gives a voting application. Those are existing contributions. Our narrower proposed comparison concerns the relation between predictive compression and this strategic abstraction. We have not established that the comparison is novel across the whole literature.

07 · Same baseline, different response

90 seconds. This tiny example is useful because it separates an observation from an interpretation. Let g indicate whether actuation is reversed and let the desired outcome be one. Under ordinary actuation, both systems choose zero and reach the target. When reversal is announced, the adaptive system chooses one while the frozen system stays at zero. Their baseline behavior is identical, but the mechanism response differs. The calculation is checked by exact enumeration. It is not new agency theory, and a simple conditional controller can realize the adaptive behavior. That is why conventional adaptive control belongs among our baselines. The example is a diagnostic, not evidence of collective or autonomous agency.

08 · What the feedback paper establishes

90 seconds. The public feedback paper is now identified correctly, with David Ehrlich, Manuel Baltieri, Fernando Rosas, and Alexander Boyd as authors. The factorization proof preserves a product belief under a particular delayed feedback construction. It does not say arbitrary correlated priors become products. Masking an exchanged token requires summing product operators, which need not remain a product. In synthetic transformers the authors report factor decoding and successful belief edits, with failures in some sns-containing settings. These results help explain a predictor’s computation. They do not by themselves identify internal goal-directed agents. We should test that extra claim instead of assuming the terminology settles it.

09 · A comparison we can actually run

90 seconds. Start small, with known coupled controllers. Include an actor–critic positive control, a frozen or replay negative control, and adaptive-control baselines. Choose candidate boundaries and outcomes first. Train or identify models on a limited intervention family, then test on held-out mechanisms and observation masks. Report error together with representation and computation costs, since a large unrestricted model can absorb many behaviors. We should not declare a collective agent solely because it fits the training data or because the microdescription is cumbersome. For equilibrium models, compare sets or specify a selection rule. This benchmark is proposed; we have not implemented the experimental comparison.

10 · What would weaken the agency interpretation?

90 seconds. The main hypothesis is that predictive compression and strategic explanatory value can rank boundaries differently. We also need to be willing to find that the agent model adds little. If a conventional adaptive model predicts equally well at comparable cost, that limits the explanatory gain from agency language. If the utility has to be rewritten after every test, the model has not generalized. A very flexible utility class can rationalize behavior without predicting much; maximum-entropy goal-directedness is a useful comparator but still depends on causal and utility choices. Boundary sensitivity can also reveal that our conclusion is an artifact of the representation. These outcomes are informative research results, not failures to tell an attractive story.

11 · The contribution is still a proposal

90 seconds. We now have a specific comparison rather than a historical narrative. The literature supplies definitions and examples. Our executed contribution so far is limited to elementary diagnostic calculations. The proposed contribution would be a controlled benchmark identifying when a compressed predictive description remains useful for strategic interventions, when it fails, and whether an agent model improves explanation. It may turn out that existing adaptive-control or abstraction methods already answer the task. Further novelty review is needed. Even a successful synthetic benchmark would not establish autonomy in living systems, institutional legitimacy, or human control. Those require additional evidence and, for authority, explicit normative commitments.

12 · Discussion

300 seconds. Use the remaining five minutes for these three questions. Start with a concrete collective the group cares about and specify one intervention whose outcome matters. Then identify a plausible non-agentic or conventional control explanation. Ask whether the learning process is inside the modeled boundary; Kenton’s account makes that consequential. If the group prefers an autonomous or institutional account, identify what extra observations would be required. End by choosing one tractable comparison, rather than agreeing only that all the papers connect.

14 · Backup: three cautions

Optional backup. Use this slide if the discussion slips from one notion of closure to another. Predictive informational closure is not the same as biological closure of maintained constraints. Mechanized abstraction preserves specified intervention semantics; it does not certify every conceivable intervention. A factorized distribution has a compact parameterization, but minimal reachable belief dimension and neural encoding dimension are different questions. None of these mathematical properties alone supplies a measure of a person’s practical authority.
