Agency across scales: speaker notes

Plan for about 15 minutes of presentation and 5 minutes of discussion. Slides 13–14 are optional backups. Timing suggestions are approximate; rehearse once and shorten transitions if needed.

1. Agency across scales

Opening, about 45 seconds. I want to connect our work on computational mechanics and neural representations to a question about agency: when do interacting components form a larger unit that can usefully be described as acting? My proposal is to approach this through interfaces and the distinctions they preserve. This is a conceptual argument with a developing formal specification. I will distinguish established source results, observations reported in the group's drafts, and my proposed extensions. The second talk will use AI islands as a concrete organizational test case. The cover is an AI-generated textile metaphor for organization across scales, not a measured network or scientific result.

2. The organizing question

About 75 seconds. A system can be composed of many interacting parts and still have a useful higher-level description. Think of a team that can revise a plan even as the people doing particular jobs change. The interesting question is what makes that description earn its place. I suggest looking at what enters and leaves the proposed unit, and what distinctions remain available when it must respond to a changed condition. This connects prediction to agency while leaving the criteria for agency open to discussion. I am using meaningful organization to mean organization that supports an explanatory and task-relevant description. This does not yet settle semantic meaning, consciousness, moral standing, or legitimate authority. This slide states the proposed synthesis, rather than a result from a single paper.

3. Process, representation, and authority

About 90 seconds. These distinctions are the backbone of both talks. First, there is the actual interacting process, including its communications and environmental effects. Second, there is an observer's representation, which might be a Bayesian belief, a neural activation, or a human-facing summary. Third, in organizations, there are rights and permissions over decisions. Hiding an exchange from an observer changes the second object. Removing the exchange changes the first. Giving a person an enforceable veto changes the third. If we change all of them at once, we cannot tell why an arrangement helped or failed. This is a modeling choice adopted for this project. It also prevents a neural observation about low-dimensional representations from silently becoming a claim about human power.

4. A higher level with a description of its own

About 90 seconds. Software in the Natural World gives a precise language for higher-level organization. Compare predicting the future macroprocess from its own history with predicting it from the underlying micro history. Informational closure says the latter adds no predictive information. The paper defines causal-state and computational forms of closure as well, with specific relationships between them. The informational-to-computational implication discussed here uses spatial coarse-graining. I would add task relevance when selecting a description for our project, because a constant macrovariable could be closed without explaining anything useful. Closure across levels is compatible with ongoing exchange across an agent-environment boundary. Sources: Rosas et al., Software in the Natural World, arXiv:2402.09090v2, PDF pp. 3–8, especially the counterfactual qualifications on pp. 4 and 6. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09090v2

5. Perception–action boundaries

About 90 seconds. Causal Blankets starts with a candidate division into a system and its environment. It then constructs minimal dynamical sufficient statistics in each direction. These supply sensory and active sides of an informational interface. They can summarize a history, so they need not look like a physical membrane. The existence result is very general: each bipartition has such a blanket. That means existence alone cannot tell us which boundary is the agent. We should compare candidate divisions at a specified timescale, ask which make a useful description, and test proposed channels. The stronger trajectory-independence result requires factorisable dynamics. The paper explicitly warns that partial observation limits counterfactual guarantees. Source: Rosas et al., Causal Blankets, arXiv:2008.12568v2, PDF pp. 3–7. https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12568v2

6. A working account of agency

About 90 seconds. This is the additional conceptual commitment I want to discuss. I would require evidence of an organized unit that persists over a relevant timescale, effective action on its surroundings, and regulation through feedback. To justify a claim at a collective scale, I would also compare the explanatory value of that collective description with a specified alternative using components separately. These are working criteria, not necessary and sufficient conditions proven by the source papers. A thermostat may satisfy a minimal form of regulation. Planning, learning, and revising goals would support stronger claims, without making them mandatory for every minimal agent. We also need to state the target being maintained and avoid inventing a goal after observing a regularity. Agency remains separate from consciousness and from any right to decide for others.

7. The question can recur at different scales

About 75 seconds. These are illustrations of how a question can recur, not evidence that the three systems instantiate the same mathematical model. In a neural system, we might perturb a candidate representation and ask what downstream behavior changes. In a controller, we might introduce an environmental disturbance. In a team, we might change the evidence or a participant and ask whether a coordinated response persists. An intervention-sensitive representation inside a predictor still does not automatically qualify as an agent. We would need the additional properties from the previous slide. The variables, feasible interventions, and outcomes change across domains. So the cross-scale connection is initially methodological: compare interfaces and responses. A stronger claim that a result transfers requires a specific mapping and evidence that the relevant mechanisms persist.

8. What the neural draft reports

About 90 seconds. The feedback draft gives the most concrete link to our group's work. In small transformers trained on known interacting processes, it reports decodable per-factor beliefs, patching experiments that move predictions toward counterfactual references, and examples where omitting an observed stream raises effective rank. The patching evidence matters: it goes beyond a decoding correlation. But we should keep the scope explicit. The evidence concerns synthetic tasks with known reference beliefs. The factors are what the predictor represents, which is different from discovering autonomous agents inside the predictor. Also, presentation dimension, belief-cloud PCA rank, and neural effective rank are different quantities. I am omitting headline dimensional numbers to keep that distinction clear. Source: supplied Beliefs in Feedback Loops draft, PDF pp. 4–9, 36–38, archived 10 November 2026, SHA256 prefix 52e8f0c44838.

9. Tests that would strengthen the connection

About 90 seconds. The next experiments should build on the draft's existing intervention tests. First, compare decoding across layers and training stages with matched decoder capacity. The draft already reports substantial decoding at random initialization, so high decoding alone is not the conclusion. Second, ask whether targeted edits have the intended effect without broad leakage into other factors. Third, compare ways the network could avoid storing a large joint table. Simon's notes describe factored storage with nonlinear reconstruction and using earlier tokens. Those are important alternative explanations. Finally, test new policy and reveal-order combinations and priors with initial dependence. The unverified propositions and preliminary approximations in Simon's draft remain unverified. Sources: feedback draft, p. 5 and pp. 24–26; Iliad_project_notes-1.pdf, pp. 7–10. These experiments have not been run in this repository.

10. Routine prediction can miss a useful correction

About 2 minutes. This is the repository's checked constructed example. Z and R are independent fair bits. Routine behavior sets the next Z to one minus Z. Switching sets the next Z to R and adds a loss of 0.2. Loss is one minus the next Z, plus that switching cost. A report that contains only Z therefore predicts the routine Z trajectory perfectly. But when Z equals one, the best action depends on R. With only Z, the expected switching loss is 0.7, so the best rule switches. It loses 0.2 in the R-equals-zero case and loses nothing relative to full information in the other three equally likely cases. Hence overall excess loss is 0.05. Ask the group briefly which missing fact changes the choice. This is a one-step decision result, not an organizational experiment and not a counterexample to an action-complete transducer. Source: output/pdf/verify_counterexample.py, exact enumeration, rechecked 18 November 2026.

11. An interface that supports correction

About 90 seconds. This is the formal direction, phrased without committing to a final theorem. If a report makes two histories look identical, we want to know whether those histories have similar consequences under feasible future policies. We must fix what a policy can observe, which actions it can take, the future horizon, and the outcomes we care about. The criterion in the appendix uses distances between outcome distributions. It is deliberately strong and may be more than we need to preserve one particular decision, so task-specific decision loss also matters. This is not a new invention of action-conditioned sufficiency. AI in a Vat and the epsilon-transducer literature already treat interfaces over action sequences. The proposed extension concerns human-usable reports and constrained correction rights. Source: Rosas, Boyd and Baltieri, AI in a Vat, archived 38-page snapshot, PDF pp. 4–6 and 10. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04608

12. The research claim to discuss

Reserve about 5 minutes for discussion, adjusting earlier slides to fit the 20-minute slot. My claim is a research connection rather than a unified theory already proved. Computational mechanics supplies candidate representations and boundaries. Neural experiments can test their implementation and use. The agency proposal adds regulation and effective action. The organizational extension asks whose ability to correct decisions survives the interface. Invite objections to the proposed agency criteria first, then ask what the smallest decisive experiment would be. If the discussion turns to human authority, bridge to the second talk: even a representation that preserves the right distinctions needs an institution that lets a person act on them. The two formal source papers do not establish moral standing or legitimate decision authority.

13. Backup: a light formal specification

Optional backup, outside the timed core. The closure expression is shorthand for the relevant future horizons, with well-defined information quantities and the source's process assumptions. The directional interface also requires A to be a function of X's history, with minimality if constructing the minimal statistic. Reverse X and Y for the sensory direction. For the proposed report requirement, define Q^sigma(.|h) using the controlled generator initialized after history h and a common policy sigma implementable through the stated information and actions. Require sup over sigma of total-variation distance between Q^sigma(.|h) and Q^sigma(.|h') to be at most epsilon whenever tau(h)=tau(h'). A finite test establishes only tested coverage. This is a proposed sufficient-information criterion, not a new theorem or an agency definition. See the companion essay for the full specification and references.
