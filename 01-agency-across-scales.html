When does a collective earn an agent model?

Core question: when does treating a collective as an agent explain its responses to change better than treating it as a predictive pattern?

A team can be predictable without pursuing a shared goal. It can pursue a shared goal while individual members have little influence over it. These possibilities motivate three separate questions: what makes a system an agent, when a group can be modeled as an agent, and whether that organization preserves people’s agency. This draft concentrates on the second. The organizational draft takes up the third; competing answers to the first constrain both.

Working thesis, not a new theorem: predictive organization supplies candidate descriptions. A collective-agent model earns additional explanatory value when a restricted account of goals and policy adaptation predicts responses to specified interventions. The research task is to compare those descriptions on the same systems and identify where they disagree.

1. Existing work already answers part of the question

Jørgensen, Weichwald, and Hammond (2026) formalize collective agency through mechanized causal models and causal abstraction. Their actor–critic example and voting application already show how to assess a higher-level agent model. We should use that framework rather than present “agency across scales” as an unaddressed problem. Hammond et al. (2023) supply the crucial distinction: changing an outcome while policies stay fixed differs from changing a mechanism that agents know about and respond to when choosing policies.

Our narrower question is whether a macrodescription selected for predictive compression is also useful for predicting that second kind of response. The literature reviewed here does not establish that predictive closure supplies this guarantee. Nor have we established that nobody has studied this comparison. A benchmark connecting the two is a candidate contribution, contingent on further novelty review and informative results.

These are competing explanatory commitments, not successive boxes on a universal checklist. Montévil and Mossio (2015) concern mutually maintained constraints; their organizational closure is distinct from closure of predictive information. Kolchinsky and Wolpert (2018) ask which system–environment correlations matter causally for a specified measure of continued existence. A collective may preserve itself while disregarding its members. Neither persistence nor a fitted utility establishes legitimate authority.

3. What computational mechanics contributes

In Rosas et al., Software in the natural world, histories belong to the same predictive state when they imply the same distribution of future observations. Informational closure asks whether the macro past predicts the macro future as well as the micro past does. This provides a disciplined account of predictive organization. A constant macrovariable also passes trivially, so the outcome being retained matters.

Causal blankets supplies minimal directional statistics across a chosen bipartition. The active side summarizes system history for predicting the environment; the sensory side does the reverse. Because the construction is available for every bipartition, blanket existence does not select an agent. Bruineberg et al. (2022) separately caution against converting a statistical modeling boundary into a physical or metaphysical conclusion without additional premises. Their critique concerns Markov-blanket interpretations; it is not a refutation of the causal-blanket construction.

The practical role of these tools is to propose and compare interfaces. We must then test actions, responses, and goals. A boundary discovered under one observation scheme can move when the available evidence or temporal grain changes.

4. World models connect prediction to possible action

Rosas, Boyd, and Baltieri, AI in a vat studies interfaces describing observations under possible action sequences. This is stronger than predicting a stream generated under one fixed policy. But an action-complete interface for a fixed environment does not automatically describe how other agents revise their policies when the rules change. Mechanized games make that adaptation explicit.

The now-public Ehrlich, Baltieri, Rosas, and Boyd (2026) gives a second bridge. Under its feedback construction and an initially factored belief, Bayesian updates preserve a product representation. Hiding exchanged tokens can break that factorization. Small transformers trained on the synthetic processes often encode and use the factors, with documented failures in some patching settings. This is evidence about how a predictor represents interacting processes. The paper’s use of transducers as “agents” does not, by itself, establish goal-directed agency in every represented factor or in the transformer.

5. A simple discrimination before a large experiment

Checked analytical illustration. Suppose a system chooses a binary action A and the outcome is Y = 1 XOR A XOR g. The desired outcome is Y = 1; g indicates whether actuation has been reversed. At g = 0, an adaptive controller A = g and a frozen controller A = 0 produce identical behavior. Once the reversal is announced, at g = 1, they separate: only the adaptive controller preserves Y = 1.

This two-regime calculation is elementary and follows the motivation of Kenton and Hammond. It is not a new discovery algorithm, a claim about consciousness, or evidence that a particular organization is an agent. Its purpose is to show why baseline predictive accuracy cannot settle adaptation. A richer learned policy might already encode both regimes; the relevant boundary and information available before policy choice must be stated. Executable calculation.

6. A research comparison that could change our minds

Proposed, not run. Begin with small coupled controllers whose mechanisms and update rules are known. Include an actor–critic system as a positive control, a frozen or replay controller as a negative control, and an ordinary adaptive control baseline. Predeclare candidate collective boundaries, outcomes, utility hypotheses, and intervention families. Compare: a macro predictor selected for compression; a goal-based mechanized macro model; and a component model with explicit adaptation. Match training information and report model size, computation, and held-out error.

ComparisonEvidence that would matter
Routine observations → announced mechanism changeDoes a small goal-based model generalize better at comparable cost, or does an ordinary adaptive model explain equally much?
Full observations → masked observationsDoes predictive dimension increase while strategic response remains simple? Keep the generator unchanged for this comparison.
Within-family → new intervention familyDoes an apparently successful macro utility fail after changing incentives or component access? Do not refit it on test outcomes.

The main hypothesis is that predictive compression and strategic explanatory value need not rank boundaries in the same order. The alternative is that, on the chosen tasks, predictive models or conventional adaptive controllers capture the relevant responses just as economically. That null result would limit the added value of agent language. MacDermott et al. (2024) offers a quantitative goal-directedness comparator, but its utility hypotheses and causal model must be fixed or evaluated on held-out cases. Flexible utility fitting can otherwise make an agency claim uninformative.

For games with several equilibria, compare sets of admissible outcomes or specify and test an equilibrium-selection rule. Do not silently replace a set-valued model with a point prediction. Any claim about transfer to organisms or institutions would require additional evidence beyond these synthetic comparisons.

7. What we can claim now

We have an evidence-backed distinction between predictive organization, strategic adaptation, autonomous self-maintenance, and institutional agency. We have a checked illustration and a comparison to run. We do not yet have a new theorem linking closure to agency, a validated agency detector, or an empirical result favoring the proposed model.

For discussion: which interventions should an agent model survive, and what predictive or explanatory advantage would justify using it? The companion develops the notation and shows exactly which pieces come from existing papers.
