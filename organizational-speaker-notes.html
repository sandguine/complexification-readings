AI islands and human control: speaker notes

Plan for about 15 minutes of presentation and 5 minutes of discussion. Slides 13–14 are optional backups. Timing suggestions are approximate; rehearse once and shorten transitions if needed.

1. AI islands and human control

Opening, about 45 seconds. I want to build on Alec's islands proposal and ask what it would actually require in an organization. The guiding outcome is whether people can notice a consequential problem and change the decision. I will describe a concrete workflow, identify what the conceptual connection supports, and propose comparisons that could favor either islands or a less restrictive arrangement. This is a design hypothesis and proposed research program. No human pilot has been run. The cover image is an AI-generated textile metaphor used as a background, not a diagram of a measured system.

2. Alec’s islands proposal

About 90 seconds. The island metaphor reverses a picture in which people depend on a central AI while losing direct relationships with each other. In the meeting, Alec proposes people communicating directly and AIs communicating through people. The supplied written draft also makes observability and understanding central, allowing an interpretation in which a direct channel could remain if it is effectively reviewed. I want to keep these variants distinct because their practical implications differ. The meeting also raises beneficial coordination, shared priors, and indirect communication. Those objections belong in the design. We should not treat the transcript as independent verification of technical claims or external incidents. Sources: The Dimensional Balance of Power, working draft dated 26 October 2026, sections 5–6; local numbered meeting transcript, lines 107–136 and 141–169. The exact meeting date is not established.

3. The conceptual link

About 90 seconds. Under the feedback draft's specified operators and an existing product-form belief, updates preserve a factored representation. Its synthetic examples also show how marginalizing an observed stream can make predictive representation more demanding. This gives us a reason to study communication interfaces. It does not directly establish a policy. Hiding a message from an observer leaves the message operating in the generator, whereas banning or rerouting it changes the process. Nor does a lower-dimensional predictor automatically become understandable to a person. We need an empirical bridge from predictive representation to usable review, and another from review to effective action. Sources: Beliefs in Feedback Loops, PDF pp. 4–9, 24–26 and 36–38. These are reported draft findings, not an organizational trial or a replication in this repository.

4. Five choices inside an island design

About 90 seconds. These choices make the proposal concrete. Observation is what the reviewer can access. Communication is how the participants actually exchange information. Reporting is the representation offered to the reviewer. Execution rights determine who can commit an action. Objectives and standing specify the values and people the arrangement serves. A shared writable document belongs in the communication specification even if nobody sends a chat message. Timing belongs in the first four choices because evidence that arrives after an irreversible action cannot support that correction. If all five change at once, an outcome difference is difficult to interpret. This separation is the project's proposed modeling discipline, informed by the source distinctions between observation and generator changes. It is not a claim that information theory chooses legitimate objectives.

5. A fictional research-budget decision

About 2 minutes. Here is one concrete island condition. An evidence assistant and a planning assistant support people allocating a fictional budget. The evidence assistant works in a private workspace. Its human partner checks a structured handoff before it becomes an input to the planner. The handoff includes sources, uncertainty, and dissenting evidence, with access to the originals. Shared writable tools are restricted or explicitly accounted for. Humans can talk directly and ask for revisions. A designated human authorizes the final decision. Now introduce a missed constraint affecting a small proposal. Can someone recover the evidence and change the allocation before authorization? That is the test. Reviewers may also lose information or become a bottleneck. A logged collaboration condition with identical final execution rights is therefore an essential comparator. This workflow is our proposed example, not Alec's fully specified protocol and not an experiment already conducted.

6. Control as an observable sequence

About 90 seconds. This is our proposed operational account of control. It is deliberately more demanding than showing a human in a workflow diagram. First, relevant information must arrive in time. Second, someone must understand enough to recognize the problem. Third, that person needs standing and an effective way to intervene. Fourth, the intervention must change the outcome. We should measure each step separately so we can diagnose the mechanism. A design might improve visibility while worsening correction because people become overloaded. We also need to name whose control we mean. A manager may gain authority while a worker or affected stakeholder loses a way to contest the decision. Measure mistaken vetoes as well as successful ones, and preserve task performance and human effort as separate outcomes. This is a project proposal rather than an established empirical finding.

7. Three initial comparisons

About 90 seconds. Begin with these three conditions on the same task family. Keep final execution rights and shared-workspace permissions constant. The inputs can be matched, but the realized messages and human attention will differ, and we should measure those differences rather than claim perfect information matching. The human-relay condition helps separate routing from substantive editing. If mediation improves correction, add a delay-matched direct-collaboration condition to see whether the gain comes simply from slowing decisions. Then study bounded delegated execution in a separate stage. Otherwise, we might attribute the benefit of requiring approval to the topology of communication. This is a staged comparison design. We have not yet selected final task materials, numerical success thresholds, or sample size. Those require feasibility work before a confirmatory study.

8. Separation can leave important dependencies

About 90 seconds. The meeting's shared-prior objection is important. Two instances may respond similarly because of common information or dependencies established before the task. That is different from discovering a hidden runtime channel. We should avoid treating shared architecture itself as proof of communication. At the same time, removing direct messages does not establish statistical independence. The feedback proof assumes an existing product form and shows how it is preserved; it does not establish that observation erases arbitrary prior dependence. We must also account for shared artifacts and environmental actions, which can carry information. Finally, a human may forward content without understanding everything it communicates. A routing requirement does not certify comprehension. Sources: meeting transcript, lines 141–169; feedback draft, PDF pp. 24–26. These are qualifications motivating tests, not a claim about any named deployed service's actual data sharing.

9. Costs and limits of islands

About 90 seconds. A design that makes some actions more predictable may also make the organization less capable. Restricting collaboration can remove useful cross-checking. Human reviewers can become overloaded, especially if a high volume of routine approvals makes the exceptional case harder to notice. Summaries can hide the very disagreement needed for correction. Local separation also leaves questions of ownership, access, and exit. A person might have a nominal veto while lacking the expertise, time, or institutional security to exercise it. These are proposed failure mechanisms to measure, not effects already demonstrated by this research. Sandy's concern in the meeting about beneficial coordination is central here: we want warranted collective action that remains contestable. Source for that discussion: numbered transcript, lines 107–110.

10. What would support or weaken the hypothesis?

About 90 seconds. The pilot should have ways to reject our preferred story. Introduce a known error, a changed constraint, or a consequential dissenting signal. Randomize task versions and condition order where feasible. Evaluate successful correction separately from detection, unnecessary vetoes, elapsed time, overall quality, and reviewer effort. After a feasibility pilot, set quantitative thresholds and sample planning before the confirmatory study. A positive result would support a mechanism in that task, not a general claim about society. If direct logged collaboration performs as well, then requiring people to relay everything may be unnecessary. If delay explains the effect, a rate or permission limit may be simpler. If mediation drops rare evidence, the interface needs revision even when average prediction looks good. These are pre-result evaluation criteria.

11. Conditional policy implications

About 90 seconds. These are conditional design proposals that could eventually become organizational requirements. Each should name its mechanism, scope, human cost, and a result that would overturn it. The first three depend on evidence about how a particular workflow fails. The last expresses a value commitment: people should retain meaningful participation and a way to contest authority. Information theory alone cannot choose that commitment. It can help us investigate whether an implementation supports it. We should also distinguish local governance proposals from claims about existing law; this talk makes no legal claim. The strongest current output is a concrete research program: specify the interface, test correction, account for costs, and retain the possibility that direct collaboration with appropriate permissions is better in some settings.

12. The decision for the research group

Reserve about 5 minutes for discussion, fitting the full presentation into a 20-minute slot. Ask Alec which mechanism is essential to the proposal and what comparison would persuade him to permit a direct AI channel. Ask the group whose corrections should count and which dissenting evidence must survive a report. My suggested first study holds final human authority fixed and compares communication and reporting arrangements. That keeps the mechanism interpretable. Then study delegation separately. These are discussion prompts, not assignments accepted by anyone. Close with the relationship to the first talk: identifying useful organization tells us something about a system's interface, while designing an institution requires deciding who can act through that interface and assessing whether the action works.

13. Backup: what remains separate

Optional backup. These distinctions address the most likely overextensions of the conceptual connection. No general theorem translates a neural PCA dimension into the capacity of a human reviewer. Likewise, a larger joint simplex is an ambient representation budget, not proof that every predictor must store that many coordinates. Simon's notes motivate nonlinear and lookback comparisons. The policy discussion should preserve the useful question about asymmetric understanding while avoiding a single complexity inequality as a safety criterion. Sources: feedback draft pp. 37–38; Simon's Iliad notes pp. 7–10; Alec's working draft sections 2–6, treated as a tentative proposal.
