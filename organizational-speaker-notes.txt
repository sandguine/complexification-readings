AI islands and human control — speaker notes

01 What must people
retain control over?
45 seconds. The first talk asked when a collective deserves an agent model. This one asks whether people can direct that collective. A group can become effective at pursuing an objective while its members become less able to question or change it. Alec’s AI-islands proposal gives us a concrete design to investigate. I will make the workflow explicit, use Lauffer’s minimal-knowledge result to clarify what reports must preserve, and separate the information problem from decision rights. The numbers in the worked example are invented and checked analytically. The organizational pilot remains a proposal.

02 The core question
75 seconds. The goal is not for everyone to reconstruct every internal interaction. It is for the right people to have evidence that can change a decision and a usable way to act on it. We need to specify whose reasons count, which decisions can be revisited, and how much time there is before execution. More information can overwhelm someone; less information can hide exactly the fact that matters. A simple summary can be sufficient for one objective and fail after an authorized objective change. This gives us a specific research question rather than a general claim that transparency or human review is always good.

03 A concrete islands workflow
90 seconds. Imagine a resource-allocation exercise with several teams. Each assistant can inspect local evidence and suggest a plan. A cross-team handoff carries not just a recommendation but its assumptions, evidence references, uncertainty, and any disagreement. A person reviews and forwards it, can request underlying evidence, and can refuse a proposed action. Final decision and execution permissions are specified separately, with an appeal route for affected people. These workflow details are our operationalization of the supplied AI-islands idea. They should not all be attributed to Alec. The important question is which part of the design creates any benefit: mediation, evidence access, or actual rights to change the outcome.

04 Do not bundle four different changes
75 seconds. This separation is the backbone of the study. Masking a message changes what someone can observe. Removing the message channel changes the interacting process. Giving someone a veto changes available actions. Changing the criterion for a good allocation changes the objective. An islands design can change all four at once, making its apparent benefit difficult to explain. First use a fixed generator to isolate the information effect. Then compare live communication arrangements with matched final rights and evidence access. Finally vary rights independently. Some interactions will remain, but stating them explicitly is much better than attributing every improvement to human mediation.

05 Who needs to know what?
75 seconds. Lauffer and coauthors give us a precise starting point. For one player, take the other participants’ possible policies and group those that induce the same entire set of best responses. Those classes retain the distinctions needed to recover that set. This is stronger than merely retaining one common optimal action. Their computational work focuses on common-payoff dynamic games, while the paper states that the theory extends to general-sum settings. We should not pretend this is a new result. Our question is how a reporting interface performs when an authorized decision context changes, and whether a human can actually infer and use the reported distinctions.

06 A summary can fail after priorities change
90 seconds. Here the co-policy can be C, D, or E, each equally likely. Under the routine objective, A is best in C and E, so one report can merge those types. Under the revised objective, E instead calls for B while C still calls for A. The old report has erased a relevant distinction. The companion gives the complete payoff table: under the revised objective, A pays four, zero, zero across C, D, E, while B pays two, three, two. With full information the expected value is three. With the old C/E report, both actions average two in that cell, giving total value seven thirds. The information loss is two thirds.

07 Less required knowledge can mean less control
90 seconds. Now remove the right to choose B. The only feasible best response is A for all types, so no information is needed to recover it. The information regret relative to full information under those same restricted rights is zero. Nevertheless, attainable value has fallen to four thirds. This shows why minimal information alone is an inadequate measure of control. One must compare both information loss within a rights arrangement and the options available across rights arrangements. The script exhausts every deterministic partition of the three types and checks the arithmetic exactly. It establishes this illustration, not a general effect size for organizational oversight.

08 What the world-model work contributes
90 seconds. The feedback paper gives a reason to worry about hiding interactions: the observer’s belief may become harder to represent. It does not prove that exposing every interaction helps a person make a decision. AI in a vat helps distinguish prediction from action-conditioned modeling. Hammond and colleagues further show why an announced rule change differs from a surprise event with policies fixed. Finally, Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven emphasize responsiveness to relevant human reasons and tracing to people with understanding and responsibility. A click on an approval screen is evidence of neither by itself. These literatures constrain the design in different ways; none alone establishes the best workflow.

09 Compare three designs
90 seconds. These three designs allow us to ask whether universal mediation adds something beyond evidence access and targeted escalation. Keep final correction rights comparable at first. Introduce routine cases, errors, cross-team inconsistencies, and an explicitly authorized change in priorities. For a replay experiment, freeze the generator and compare reports. For live interaction, allow the design to affect delay and strategic adaptation, and measure those effects. A report can be refreshed after a context changes if evidence and time remain available; this is a serious alternative to requiring every future-relevant distinction in the initial handoff. We should compare fixed and adaptive reporting rather than make the compressed baseline artificially weak.

10 Measure correction, cost, and influence
90 seconds. Correction success is central, but it should not be measured without costs. A workflow can stop errors by stopping useful work too. Record false interventions, delay, workload, and whether source evidence was actually accessible. Also report whose objections change outcomes and who can revise the objective. Where stakeholders disagree, do not silently combine them into one team payoff. The rival hypothesis is that direct logged collaboration with selective escalation provides the same correction capacity more cheaply. If so, the general case for universal islands weakens. We have not justified numerical success thresholds or a sample size; the toy values are not empirical estimates for power analysis.

11 A conditional organizational implication
90 seconds. The recommendation we can currently defend is to specify what people must be able to challenge, preserve access to evidence that could change those judgments, and test whether their interventions really affect execution. Human mediation may be useful when it exposes omitted evidence and prompts effective challenge. It can fail through rubber-stamping, lack of expertise, bottlenecks, or corrupted handoffs. Separate channels can still share model errors and incentives. If a benefit comes entirely from an enforceable veto, that supports the veto rather than universal message review. Our task is to identify those mechanisms and the conditions under which they work.

12 Discussion
300 seconds. Reserve five minutes for these questions. Ask the group to pick a concrete allocation or coordination decision. Identify the affected people and which reasons should count, rather than assuming a single manager is the only relevant human. Then specify one correction that the workflow must permit and the evidence needed to justify it. Finally ask what result would lead us to prefer logged collaboration or selective escalation instead of islands. This should produce a bounded pilot question and an explicit authority assumption, not a general endorsement of human oversight.

13 Backup: complete payoff table
Optional backup. The routine partition is C/E versus D. The revised partition is C versus D/E. Their common refinement has three singleton cells. With a fixed report serving both contexts, all three types must be distinguished to recover the full best-response set. This is an elementary common-refinement construction based on Lauffer’s SER, not a new theorem. For another task, preserving one common optimal action can need fewer distinctions than recovering the entire set; the companion gives an overlapping-set example.
