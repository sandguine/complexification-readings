What must people retain control over?

Core question: which information and decision rights let people correct AI-assisted collective decisions when circumstances or authorized objectives change?




A collective can become more effective at pursuing a goal while its members become less able to change what it does. The first draft asks when an agent model explains the collective. This draft asks what would preserve human direction of it. Neither collective intelligence nor a compact world model answers that second question.


Working hypothesis: useful oversight depends on preserving distinctions relevant to authorized corrections, together with effective rights to act on them. Human mediation of every AI message is one possible design, whose benefits and costs need comparison.


1. Make the AI-islands proposal concrete


The supplied XOR Labs working draft, The Dimensional Balance of Power, associated with Alec’s presentation, motivates separating AI assistants into islands with human-mediated exchanges. The following workflow is our operationalization, not a claim that Alec specified every detail.


Consider a fictional resource-allocation team. Each team member has an assistant that can inspect local materials and propose an allocation. A cross-team handoff includes the proposal, evidence links, uncertainty, assumptions, and disagreements. A person can revise or reject it, request the underlying evidence, and approve its transmission. A designated decision maker can revise the final plan before execution; affected team members have a specified appeal route. Assistants have bounded execution permissions.


That arrangement changes communication and authority. Merely hiding AI messages from a dashboard changes observation. Deleting an AI-to-AI channel changes the process itself. Assigning a person a veto changes available actions. Revising the allocation criterion changes the normative objective. These changes need separate descriptions and, where feasible, separate comparisons.


2. Coordination tells us what a summary must preserve


Lauffer, Shah, Carroll, Dennis, and Russell (2023) define strategic equivalence: two co-policies are equivalent for a player if they induce the same entire set of best responses. The resulting partition identifies distinctions needed to recover that set. Their computational development focuses on common-payoff dynamic games; the paper states that the underlying theory extends to general-sum games.


This is a better starting point than requiring every person to reconstruct the whole system. But it is relative to a decision problem. A summary adequate for one objective and one set of available actions may fail when either changes. Preserving the whole best-response set is also stronger than preserving just one common optimal action. The mathematical companion keeps those requirements separate.


Our proposed extension is deliberately modest: evaluate the same reporting interface across a declared family of authorized decision contexts. The resulting partition is a common refinement of the existing task-specific partitions—an elementary construction, not a claimed new theorem. The open research problem is how to approximate and learn useful reports under changing contexts, limited human attention, and disagreement about goals.

For the routine objective, C and E can be summarized together: both call for A. D calls for B. After an authorized change in the objective, E calls for B while C still calls for A. With complete information, expected value is 3. Using the old C/E summary, the best achievable expected value is 7/3: inside that cell, A and B each average 2. The information loss costs 2/3.


Now remove the right to choose B. Every co-policy has the same feasible best response, A, so a constant report preserves the whole feasible best-response set. Yet expected value under the revised objective is only 4/3. Less information is “needed” partly because the person has lost a consequential option. This is why reporting quality and authority must be evaluated separately. The exact enumeration checks all five deterministic partitions of the three co-policies. It is an illustration, not a study of human behavior.


4. Why lower predictive dimension is not the policy objective


Ehrlich et al. (2026) shows that masking interactions can disrupt factorized beliefs in a predictor. That supports concern about losing useful structure. It does not imply that showing every interaction maximizes human control. A task-relevant summary can discard much predictive detail without changing a decision. Conversely, a compact summary can preserve the wrong objective perfectly.


Rosas et al., AI in a vat clarifies why modeling available actions matters. Hammond et al. (2023) adds that announcing a new rule can change the policies of the other participants. An oversight test must therefore distinguish a surprise event under fixed policies from an announced change that agents can adapt to. A passive replay of a transcript cannot establish performance under both.


Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018) provide a complementary normative account: meaningful human control requires responsiveness to relevant human reasons and a connection to humans who understand the system and their responsibility. These are proposed necessary conditions, not a validated measure for our pilot. A record of someone clicking “approve” establishes neither. We must also say whose reasons count; management preference, worker interests, and affected people’s claims may diverge.


5. Compare mechanisms before recommending islands


Proposed study, not run. Use the same fictional allocation tasks and outcome criteria in three designs:

Delegation with escalationRoutine exchanges proceed; specified conflicts or uncertainty trigger review.Match final rights; vary when attention is requested.



First isolate reporting in a computational or replay task with the generator and rights held fixed. Then compare live communication arrangements, where mediation can alter timing and policy adaptation. Finally vary correction rights independently. This staging prevents a gain caused by a new veto from being attributed to message mediation, or a loss caused by delay from being attributed to compression.


Include routine cases, concealed errors, cross-team inconsistencies, and an explicitly authorized change in priorities. Match model version, task resources, training, and evidence access. Measure correction success before execution, utility regret relative to a stated information benchmark, false interventions, time, workload, and whose objections affect the result. Where objectives conflict, report outcomes separately rather than quietly collapsing them into one team score.


6. Predictions, alternatives, and failure conditions


Islands may help when reviewing a handoff exposes missing evidence, allows a person to challenge a local assumption, and changes the eventual action. They may fail when reviewers rubber-stamp, lack expertise, cannot access the original evidence, or become bottlenecks. Humans can also forward errors between islands. Shared models and incentives can create correlated failures despite separated message channels.


The main prediction is that context-relevant reports plus usable correction rights improve correction under changed conditions, at an acceptable attention cost. The rival explanation is that logged collaboration with targeted escalation achieves equal or better correction more quickly. If that happens, the case for universal human mediation weakens. If islands improve routine accuracy but worsen correction after a priority change, they have failed the central test.


No numerical success threshold or sample size has yet been justified. The next protocol must specify them from task stakes, variance estimates, and what improvement would be practically meaningful, before confirmatory data collection. The toy payoff gaps are not empirical effect-size estimates.


7. The organizational implication we can defend


Specify the decisions people must be able to revisit, preserve access to evidence that can change those decisions, and test whether their interventions actually take effect. Treat the reporting map, communication process, permissions, and objective as separate design choices. This is a conditional research recommendation; the evidence reviewed here does not establish that AI islands generally outperform alternatives.


For discussion: what must an affected person be able to notice, challenge, and change—and which design achieves that with tolerable burden?
