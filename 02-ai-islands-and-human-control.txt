AI islands and human control: a design hypothesis

AI islands could help if their boundaries give people usable opportunities to understand, challenge, and redirect collective decisions. Whether they do so depends on the information people receive, the time they have, and the authority they can exercise.

Organizational and policy proposals for testing. No organizational trial or general safety guarantee is claimed.

What I take from Alec’s proposal

Alec’s presentation imagines AIs as islands within a connected human society: people retain direct relationships with one another, while exchanges among AIs pass through interfaces people can observe. The meeting’s first formulation emphasizes preventing direct AI-to-AI communication. The supplied written draft also considers a weaker requirement: observable and understood communication. These are related designs with different costs. [A, §§5–6; B, lines 111–136]

The idea connects to computational mechanics through the cost of understanding an interacting system. The supplied feedback paper gives conditions under which an observer can retain separate predictive beliefs about its components. It reports synthetic examples where hiding an exchange makes that representation more demanding. This motivates investigating communication interfaces as an organizational design variable. It does not establish that banning communication improves human control. [C, PDF pp. 4–9, 24–26, 36–38]

I would preserve Alec’s concern about asymmetric visibility and human dependence, while recasting islands as a family of institutional arrangements. The practical question becomes: which arrangement lets people detect an important problem and successfully correct it, at an acceptable cost?

What an island would mean in practice

A useful specification has to distinguish the channel through which AIs exchange information from the permissions through which they affect the world. It also has to account for shared documents and tools, which can carry information even when direct messages are absent.

Design choice | Concrete implementation | What it changes |

Observation | Expose an existing exchange to a reviewer, with its context and source links. | The reviewer’s information. The underlying exchange can remain unchanged. |

Communication | Require a reviewed handoff before another AI receives a result, or remove a direct channel. | The process itself, including coordination possibilities and delays. |

Reporting | Provide a structured summary with disagreement, evidence links, and a route to inspect the original. | Which distinctions a person receives and can recover. |

Execution authority | Allow drafts but require approval for a commitment, with enforceable limits on delegated tools. | Who can take consequential actions and how those actions can be stopped. |

Objectives and standing | Specify whose interests count and who can challenge the decision. | The normative basis of the arrangement, independent of predictive efficiency. |

These choices should not be collapsed into a single “human in the loop” label. A person who forwards a message without understanding it has supplied a route. A person who understands a message but cannot stop execution has supplied review. Neither arrangement by itself demonstrates effective control.

A worked example: a research team allocating a small budget

Consider a proposed low-stakes pilot in which a team allocates a fictional research budget. An evidence assistant reviews proposals. A planning assistant recommends an allocation. Human participants agree on the evaluation criteria and can change them. No real funds move during the pilot.

In the island condition, the evidence assistant writes to a private workspace. Its human partner reviews a handoff containing the recommendation, supporting sources, uncertainty, and any dissenting evidence. The planning assistant receives the approved handoff through a controlled input. Shared writable files and other tool routes are restricted or explicitly recorded. Human participants can speak directly, inspect the original evidence, and ask either assistant to reconsider. A designated human decision-maker authorizes the final allocation.

Suppose the evidence assistant misses a constraint affecting a small proposal. The review should let someone recover that evidence, challenge the recommendation, and obtain a revised plan before authorization. The practical test is whether the constraint survives the interface and changes the outcome. Simply recording that a human clicked “approve” would not answer that question.

Even this design has a cost. People may become a bottleneck, lose context when summarizing, or rely too heavily on the first recommendation. Restricting AI collaboration could also prevent useful cross-checking. A direct, logged collaboration condition with the same execution permissions is therefore an essential comparator.

What “people stay in control” should mean

For this project, I propose evaluating control through a sequence of observable opportunities. A person receives relevant evidence in time, understands enough to recognize a problem, has standing and a feasible means to challenge it, and can cause an effective revision before the relevant consequence becomes irreversible. Record where this sequence fails.

Measure successful correction separately from error detection. Also measure mistaken vetoes, delay, task quality, and human effort. Where several people are affected, record whose objection receives attention. An arrangement can strengthen a manager’s control while weakening that of employees or other affected people. “Human control” therefore needs a named subject and a stated scope.

This links to the first essay’s proposed interface criterion: a summary should preserve the distinctions needed for the available corrective actions. Action rights remain a separate variable. A report might faithfully preserve every relevant fact while the organization gives its recipient no power to act on them.

Where islands could fall short

Visibility can exceed comprehension. An exchange can be logged and still be too long, specialized, or ambiguous to review. A summary can be readable while omitting the rare fact that matters. Human mediation is therefore a hypothesis about usable oversight, not a certificate of semantic transparency.

Common causes can survive separation. Shared training, common inputs, or correlated initialization can produce similar behavior without a fresh message. The meeting raised precisely this concern. Identical software alone does not imply a secret runtime channel, but a ban on messages does not establish independent behavior either. The factorization proof in the supplied draft preserves an existing product form under specified operators; it does not show that observation eliminates arbitrary initial dependence. [B, lines 141–169; C, PDF pp. 24–26]

Coordination can move elsewhere. Shared files, external tools, and changes in the environment can transmit information. The proposed island boundary must state which of these paths exist. A human relay can itself transmit information the reviewer does not understand. Removing a chat edge from a diagram establishes little about these other routes.

Isolation can reduce beneficial coordination. Separate assistants may duplicate work, miss contradictions, or produce incompatible plans. The user’s concern in the meeting about beneficial coordination is central: the objective is to preserve warranted collective action while keeping it contestable. Increased coordination and decreased human agency can occur together, so evaluate both. [B, lines 107–110]

Local islands can conceal concentrated power. Separate assistant windows may still depend on one provider or one institution’s rules. A local communication protocol does not by itself create exit options, plural ownership, or legitimate authority. Those require additional institutional choices.

A comparison that could teach us something

Start with a small comparison at fixed final human authority and the same fictional task:

Condition | Interaction | Question |

Logged collaboration | AIs exchange results directly. People can inspect exchanges and source evidence. | How much correction can ordinary review support? |

Human relay | The person reads and forwards the same handoff before the next AI proceeds. | Does the review gate help, and is the effect just additional time? |

Human-edited handoff | The person checks and edits a structured report, retaining access to the original evidence. | Does active mediation preserve the evidence needed for correction? |

Use the same case inputs and the same permissions over final execution. Log actual message contents, access to evidence, time, and effort rather than pretending that different communication routes produce identical information. Keep shared-workspace permissions constant in the first comparison. Add a delay-matched collaboration condition if mediation helps, to distinguish a review effect from a slowdown effect.

Then vary bounded delegation separately, so a communication effect is not confused with a change in authority. Introduce a known mistaken assumption, a changed constraint, or a consequential dissenting signal. Randomize task versions and condition order where feasible, and evaluate correction against criteria specified in advance. Set numerical success thresholds and sample size after a feasibility pilot, before the confirmatory study.

The islands hypothesis gains support if mediation improves successful correction within agreed performance and effort limits. It weakens if logged collaboration performs as well, if delay alone explains the benefit, if reviewers miss more rare signals, or if coordination losses outweigh the improvement. A pilot should be able to favor a less restrictive design.

What this suggests for policy discussion now

The current work supports a research agenda and candidate governance requirements. It does not yet support a universal prohibition on AI-to-AI communication. An organization could ask deployments to demonstrate usable evidence access, a named correction authority, and an enforceable opportunity to intervene before specified commitments. These are proposed institutional requirements, with their costs and effectiveness still to be tested.

Conditional proposal | Reason and cost | Finding that would revise it |

Require reviewed handoffs for decisions where mediation improves correction. | Creates an opportunity to challenge a plan, at a cost in reviewer time and latency. | Logged or delay-matched collaboration yields equal correction with less effort. |

Preserve evidence access and an exception route when summaries lose consequential facts. | Makes omitted or dissenting evidence recoverable, with privacy and attention costs. | A simpler interface retains the same correction performance and coverage. |

Bound delegation when execution outruns review. | Preserves time to intervene, at a cost in speed and autonomy. | Another control achieves the same recovery reliability with lower delay. |

Protect direct human deliberation and ways to contest or leave an arrangement. | Expresses a normative commitment to human participation and plural authority. | Revise the implementation through affected people’s deliberation. Predictive accuracy alone cannot settle the value commitment. |

The bridge across scales is therefore specific. Computational mechanics helps us ask which interfaces preserve relevant information. Neural studies suggest ways to investigate whether a system represents and uses that information. Organizational design adds the question of who can act on it. AI islands are one candidate answer to that last problem, and their value should turn on whether people can actually redirect collective behavior.

Discussion questions and status

For Alec: Which part of the islands proposal is essential: preventing unreviewed communication, creating review time, preserving human-to-human deliberation, or limiting execution? Which comparison would persuade us to permit a direct AI channel?

For Simon and David: What survives correlated priors, delayed observation, and access to earlier messages? Which neural interventions distinguish a useful predictive factor from a subsystem with an agent-like role?

For the group: Whose corrections should the institution honor? Which disagreements must a report retain, even when those disagreements add little to average predictive accuracy?

These are invitations to discussion, not assignments accepted by collaborators. The essays and presentation drafts are complete. The controlled transducer study, neural replication, and human pilot remain proposed. The pilot sketch is not a deployment-ready protocol: participant procedures, final task materials, numerical decision criteria, and sample planning still need development.

Rosas’s world-model work makes the action question explicit

AI in a vat models the interface between possible actions and their outcomes. It distinguishes a generative world model, a belief representation, and a minimal predictive model. This is the strongest technical bridge from predictive organization to the information needed for correction. From monoliths to modules, coauthored by Fernando E. Rosas, adds composition and decomposition of such world models. Its cited version concerns feedforward, mechanistically stationary networks and explicitly leaves feedback, adaptation, and empirical validation open. Applying these tools to human–AI organizations is our proposed extension.

See Rosas, Boyd, and Baltieri (2025), PDF pp. 4–6, 9–10 and Boyd, Nowak, Hyland, Baltieri, and Rosas (2026), PDF pp. 3–5, 22–25. The mathematical companion works through a transducer kernel, a belief update, and the assumptions behind modular representations.
