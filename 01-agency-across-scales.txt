Agency across scales: boundaries, representations, and the capacity to act

Computational mechanics helps identify which distinctions organize a system’s behavior. Neural-network studies can test whether a model represents and uses those distinctions. Describing the resulting organization as an agent requires a further account of how it acts and regulates outcomes.

Conceptual synthesis with a developing formal proposal. Main argument followed by optional technical and evidence appendices.

Full citations and reading guide · Mathematical companion (PDF) · LaTeX source. The companion explains the notation and each mathematical step.

The connection I want to make

A neural network, an organism, and an organization all contain interacting parts. Sometimes a description at a larger scale captures something stable and useful: a memory being retrieved, a coordinated response, or a team revising a plan. My question is what would justify treating that larger pattern as a unit with a capacity to act.

I propose to approach this through interfaces: the distinctions through which a system receives information, changes its surroundings, and sustains a pattern of behavior. This gives a route between computational mechanics and agency without assuming in advance that every module is an agent. It also gives us a practical question for interpretability: which internal distinctions matter when the system changes its behavior?

The research program connects three objects that need separate treatment. There is the interacting process itself. There is an observer’s representation of that process, including a neural predictor’s learned representation. And, in a human organization, there is an allocation of decision rights. A simpler representation can help someone understand a process. Whether that person can redirect it depends on additional capabilities and authority.

When a higher level has a useful description of its own

Computational mechanics groups histories that imply the same predictive distribution into causal states. The central idea is to retain the differences in the past that matter for the future. This creates a disciplined way to ask whether an apparent pattern supports a useful model. “Causal” here has a specific predictive meaning, with stronger intervention interpretations requiring additional assumptions. [1, PDF pp. 3–4]

Software in the Natural World applies this approach across levels. For a chosen macroprocess, informational closure asks whether its own history provides all the predictive information available from the microscopic history. The paper also defines causal-state closure and computational closure, and establishes relationships under its stated conditions. In particular, the implication from informational to computational closure discussed here applies to spatial coarse-grainings. [1, PDF pp. 5–8]

This suggests a way to recognize meaningful higher-level organization: identify a macrodescription that captures relevant behavior and remains useful across the perturbations we care about. The second part is a proposed research requirement, beyond checking prediction in one observed regime. An invariant constant would be predictively closed but unhelpful for most tasks. We therefore need to state what the macrodescription retains, what it leaves out, and the conditions under which it works.

Closure also concerns the relationship between descriptions at different scales. It does not require a candidate agent to stop exchanging information with its environment. A system can have a useful higher-level organization precisely because it responds to environmental inputs through a structured interface.

How to identify a perception–action boundary

Causal Blankets offers a complementary construction. Begin with a proposed division into a system and its environment. Ask which distinctions in the environment’s history help predict the system’s next state, given the system’s own history. Ask the corresponding question in the other direction. The resulting minimal dynamical statistics provide sensory and active sides of an informational interface. These can summarize histories rather than coincide with a visible physical surface. [2, PDF pp. 4–6]

This is powerful because it makes boundaries investigable. However, the paper constructs a causal blanket for every bipartition. Existence alone therefore cannot select a uniquely correct agent. Its stronger result about independent trajectories conditioned on the blanket requires factorisable dynamics. The authors also explicitly limit counterfactual interpretations under partial observation. [2, PDF pp. 3, 6–7]

My proposed procedure is to compare candidate boundaries at a stated timescale and task: estimate the predictive interface, check what remains unexplained, and perturb the proposed sensory or active channels. A useful boundary should reveal how the system responds and should improve our explanation relative to plausible alternative partitions. In finite data, these are tests of an approximation over specified conditions, not a discovery certificate for the system’s one true boundary.

What would make that organization agent-like?

I would use a graded, task-relative account of agency. The following are working criteria for this project, rather than a theorem derived from either paper:

Candidate property | What would count as evidence? |

Continuity of the organized unit | A comparable interface and organization persist over the timescale of the task, despite some changes in components or inputs. |

Effective action | Changing the candidate’s outputs changes relevant environmental outcomes under an explicit intervention model. |

Regulation through feedback | The system uses information to maintain or recover a specified target or viable range when conditions change. Compare it with a fixed or open-loop response. |

Explanatory value at this scale | The collective description captures coordinated responses that a chosen collection of separate component models misses, at a measured explanatory or resource cost. |

A thermostat illustrates a minimal form of feedback regulation. More flexible goal revision, learning, and planning would support richer agency claims, but should not be built into a definition that excludes every simple controller. Conversely, a whirlpool may be a stable macrostructure without meeting the proposed goal-sensitive regulation criterion. These are conceptual examples, not empirical classifications established here.

For a team, a candidate collective capacity could be maintaining a plan while replacing a member and revising the plan when evidence changes. That capacity can coexist with disagreement among members. A collective agency claim also leaves open whether its decisions are legitimate, whether participation is voluntary, and whose interests it serves. Neither predictive closure nor successful regulation settles moral status or political authority.

What neural-network findings contribute

The supplied Beliefs in Feedback Loops draft gives a concrete bridge. In small transformers trained on synthetic interacting processes, it reports decodable representations of separate predictive factors and interventions on those representations that change predictions toward specified counterfactual targets. It also reports settings in which hiding part of the observed stream increases the effective rank of the learned representation. These are results reported by the draft, not replications performed in this repository. [3, PDF pp. 4–9, 36–38]

The conceptual opportunity is that an observer may learn a useful decomposition of an interacting system, and we can test which parts of that decomposition the observer actually uses. This would connect statistical structure to mechanistic interpretability. It does not establish that the represented factors are agents inside the transformer. A representation of another system’s state and a subsystem with its own capacity to act are different candidates.

The draft already includes intervention evidence, so the next step should build on it: test selectivity across layers and new regimes, compare trained and randomly initialized models, and measure leakage into other factors. Decoding alone needs care because the draft reports substantial decoding performance at initialization. Simon’s working notes add another important comparison: a model can reconstruct information using earlier tokens or nonlinear computation rather than store a large joint table in its current activation. [3, PDF p. 5; 4, PDF pp. 7–10]

The distinction that connects this to human control

A representation that predicts routine behavior may omit information needed for an available correction. The repository’s checked example makes this precise. Let Z and R be independent fair bits. Routine behavior sets the next Z to 1 − Z. Switching instead sets it to R and incurs a cost of 0.2. A report containing only Z predicts the routine Z trajectory perfectly, yet at Z = 1 it cannot tell when switching is worth its cost. Its best decision rule incurs 0.05 more expected loss overall than a rule that also observes R. [5]

This is a constructed one-step decision example, not evidence about organizations. Its point is limited and useful: predictive success depends on the regime and target being predicted. The missing R matters once a switch is available. Action-conditioned interfaces are already central to transducer work, including AI in a Vat. Our contribution would be to ask what happens when those interfaces must be usable by people with bounded attention and specified correction rights. [6, PDF pp. 4–6, 10]

My working thesis is that agency across scales becomes more tractable when we study which interfaces preserve the capacity to respond under intervention. Computational mechanics proposes representations and boundaries. Neural experiments investigate their implementation and causal use. Organizational research asks whether people can use the resulting interfaces to notice mistakes and change decisions. Each step needs its own evidence, but the questions now connect.

Optional appendix: the mathematical direction

These expressions locate the formal work. They do not yet constitute a unified theory of agency.

1. Predictive closure, following [1]

For a microprocess X and macroprocess Z obtained by a stated coarse-graining, a shorthand for informational closure is:

I(Zfuture; Xpast | Zpast) = 0

Require this for the future horizons covered by the claim, with well-defined information quantities. The source’s formal framework assumes stationary discrete processes. For empirical work, specify a horizon, approximation tolerance, estimation method, and retained task information.

2. A directional interface, following [2]

If X is the candidate system and Y its environment, the active-side statistic A is a function of X’s history satisfying:

Yt+1 ⟂ X≤t | (At, Y≤t)

The sensory-side statistic reverses X and Y. Minimality is relative to the selected bipartition and conditional predictive distributions. This conditional independence alone does not authorize replacing the observed process with an arbitrary intervention.

3. A proposed report criterion

Fix the controlled generator, history h, report τ(h), horizon H, outcome vector, and a family Σ of policies that can use only the allowed reports, future observations, and actions. Let Qσ(· | h) be the resulting outcome law when the same feasible policy σ runs after h. Consider:

τ(h) = τ(h′) impliessupσ ∈ Σ dTV(Qσ(· | h), Qσ(· | h′)) ≤ ε

This is a proposed sufficient-information test, not a novelty claim. History coverage, report updates, policy feasibility, horizon, and tolerance must be fixed. Exact enumeration is possible in a small known model. Organizational logs alone do not generally identify these laws. The condition is demanding and is not necessary for preserving one particular optimal action. Test task-specific decision regret alongside it. Keep action rights fixed for this comparison, then separately study changes in authority. No new regret theorem is asserted here.

Claim and assumption ledger

Claim | Status and source | Boundary of the claim |

Some macrodescriptions support self-contained predictive and computational structure. | Existing formal results. [1], PDF pp. 5–8. | Specified coarse-graining and process assumptions. No agency or legitimacy theorem. |

Minimal dynamical interfaces exist for each bipartition. | Existing formal result. [2], PDF pp. 4–7. | Does not uniquely select a system boundary. Strong trajectory decoupling requires factorisable dynamics. |

Small trained networks represent and use predictive factors. | Reported draft experiments. [3], PDF pp. 5–9. | Synthetic processes, known reference beliefs. No independent replication here or general frontier-model claim. |

Product-form updates can preserve factored beliefs. | Formal result in a working draft. [3], PDF pp. 24–26. | Requires the relevant existing product form, kernels, and timing. Seeing messages need not erase prior correlations. |

Routine prediction can omit correction-relevant information. | Completed analytical example. [5]. | Four equally likely states, one decision, stated loss. No empirical generalization. |

Persistent organization, effective action, and feedback regulation support a graded agency attribution. | Proposed conceptual criteria in this essay. | Need competing models and task definitions. No universal necessity or sufficiency claim. |

Interfaces preserving corrective decisions can help human control. | Research hypothesis. | Requires usable information, time, effective authority, and a separately justified objective. |

What remains open

How should we compare candidate boundaries without selecting the task only to favor one? Which perturbations distinguish regulation from a coincidental regularity? Can a compact report preserve rare but consequential distinctions? How do the costs of human comprehension compare with a machine’s predictive resources? These are the next research questions, rather than gaps hidden by an agency score.

Rosas’s world-model work makes the action question explicit

AI in a vat models the interface between possible actions and their outcomes. It distinguishes a generative world model, a belief representation, and a minimal predictive model. This is the strongest technical bridge from predictive organization to the information needed for correction. From monoliths to modules, coauthored by Fernando E. Rosas, adds composition and decomposition of such world models. Its cited version concerns feedforward, mechanistically stationary networks and explicitly leaves feedback, adaptation, and empirical validation open. Applying these tools to human–AI organizations is our proposed extension.

See Rosas, Boyd, and Baltieri (2025), PDF pp. 4–6, 9–10 and Boyd, Nowak, Hyland, Baltieri, and Rosas (2026), PDF pp. 3–5, 22–25. The mathematical companion works through a transducer kernel, a belief update, and the assumptions behind modular representations.
